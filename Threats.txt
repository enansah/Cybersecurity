A computer virus attaches itself to programs 
or files to spread and disrupt operations, while
 modern terminology broadly classifies harmful 
 software as malware.


The Brain Virus (1986)

1.Created initially to protect medical software from 
piracy, it unintentionally infected computers and 
spread globally via floppy disks within months.
2.The incident highlighted the need for strategic 
security planning by significantly slowing business 
operations and productivity.


The Morris Worm (1988)

1. Designed to measure the size of the internet, the worm 
continuously re-infected machines until memory was exhausted, 
crashing roughly 10% of connected systems.
2.The disruption resulted in millions of dollars in damages and 
led directly to the creation of Computer Emergency Response Teams 
(CERTs) to manage security incidents.


The LoveLetter Attack and Social Engineering

The LoveLetter malware spread via email attachments to steal user 
credentials, infecting 45 million computers and causing over $10 
billion in damages.
It stands as an early major example of social engineering, which manipulates
 human error to access sensitive information and often takes the 
 form of phishing emails today.


he Equifax Data Breach

In 2017, attackers breached credit agency Equifax, compromising the 
personal and financial data of over 143 million people.
The breach resulted from multiple known vulnerabilities that the company 
failed to fix, culminating in more than $575 million in government settlements 
and fines. 



Phishing
Phishing is the use of digital communications to trick people into revealing 
sensitive data or deploying malicious software.

Types

1.Business Email Compromise (BEC): A threat actor sends an email message that 
seems to be from a known source to make a seemingly legitimate request for information, 
in order to obtain a financial advantage.

2.Spear phishing: A malicious email attack that targets a specific user or group of users. 
The email seems to originate from a trusted source.

3.Whaling: A form of spear phishing. Threat actors target company executives to gain 
access to sensitive data.

4.Vishing: The exploitation of electronic voice communication to obtain sensitive information 
or to impersonate a known source.

5.Smishing: The use of text messages to trick users, in order to obtain sensitive information or 
to impersonate a known source.


Malware is software designed to harm devices or networks.

Types

1.Viruses: Malicious code written to interfere with computer operations and cause damage to data 
and software. A virus needs to be initiated by a user (i.e., a threat actor), who transmits the 
virus via a malicious attachment or file download.

2.Worms: Malware that can duplicate and spread itself across systems on its own. In contrast to 
a virus, a worm does not need to be downloaded by a user. Instead, it self-replicates and spreads 
from an already infected computer to other devices on the same network.

3.Ransomware: A malicious attack where threat actors encrypt an organization's data and demand 
payment to restore access. 

4.Spyware: Malware that’s used to gather and sell information without consent. Spyware can be used 
to access devices. This allows threat actors to collect personal data, such as private emails, texts, 
voice and image recordings, and locations.


Social engineering is a manipulation technique that exploits human error to gain private information, 
access, or valuables.

Types

1.Social media phishing: A threat actor collects detailed information about their target from social media 
sites. Then, they initiate an attack.

2.Watering hole attack: A threat actor attacks a website frequently visited by a specific group of users.

3.USB baiting: A threat actor strategically leaves a malware USB stick for an employee to find and install, 
to unknowingly infect a network. 

4.Physical social engineering: A threat actor impersonates an employee, customer, or vendor to obtain 
unauthorized access to a physical location.
